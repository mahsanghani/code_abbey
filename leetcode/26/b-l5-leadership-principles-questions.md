# (b) Leadership Principles at the L5 Level — Research + Question Bank

## What changes at L5 vs. L4 (this is the part worth internalizing)

The research is consistent on one point: **L5 isn't "the same LP questions, asked more strictly." The *content* of a good answer changes shape.**

- **Scope and ambiguity go up.** *"L5/SDE II stories should involve more ambiguity and cross-team coordination than L4 stories."* University projects and small, well-defined tasks are largely off the table — *"for L5, they look for impact, so university projects are mostly out of the equation."*
- **Your stories should show design judgment, not just execution.** *"For L5, try to make sure your LP answers reflect design decisions, design comparisons, and some technical decision-making — while for L4, make sure your answers reflect learning good coding practices, delivering implementations on time, etc."* L4 = "I did the task well." L5 = "I decided *what* the task should be and defended the tradeoff."
- **The "can my manager hand me an ambiguous problem and trust me to scope it myself" bar.** This is the single cleanest way to think about the L5 shift: *"At SDE1, someone else does the scoping. At SDE2, that's your job."* Every story should demonstrate **you** doing the scoping, not receiving it.
- **Reflection is graded, explicitly.** *"'What would you change if you did it again?' separates good answers from great ones."* This maps directly to the worksheet's "R – Reflect" step and the "create two alternative solutions" exercise — that's not busywork, it's rehearsing the exact follow-up you'll get live.
- **Every round carries LP weight, and it eats more time than expected.** Behavioral questioning is reported to consume more than half of every round, phone screen included, with interviewers going **3–5 follow-ups deep** on a single story (especially the Bar Raiser). A shallow STAR answer that can't survive five "why" and "what if" follow-ups will get exposed.

## L5-calibrated questions per LP

For each, questions are phrased at the ambiguity/scope level L5 interviewers actually ask — use these to stress-test the stories you write in the worksheet.

### Customer Obsession
- Tell me about a time you pushed back on a business or product request because it wasn't good for the customer. What was the pressure to comply, and how did you resolve it?
- Describe a time you had to weigh a business/technical need against customer impact. What did you decide, and why was it *your* call to make?
- Tell me about a time you discovered your team's product wasn't actually meeting a customer need you'd assumed was solved. What did you do?

### Ownership
- Tell me about a time you fixed something that wasn't part of your job, on a system you didn't own. What made you decide it was worth it?
- Describe a decision you made that traded short-term convenience for long-term value, even though it cost you time/political capital in the moment.
- Tell me about a project you saw through even after the person who assigned it moved on or lost interest. What kept you accountable?

### Invent and Simplify
- Tell me about a time you significantly simplified a complex system or process. What was the "not invented here" resistance you faced, if any?
- Describe a time your innovative idea was initially misunderstood or rejected. How did you validate it or bring others along?

### Insist on the Highest Standards
- Tell me about a time you refused to ship something the team considered "good enough." What was the cost of holding the line, and was it worth it?
- Describe how you've made sure a defect or problem, once fixed, *stayed* fixed (a mechanism, not a one-time patch).

### Bias for Action
- Tell me about a calculated risk you took with incomplete information. What made you confident the decision was reversible enough to act on?
- Describe a time speed mattered more than certainty. How did you avoid analysis paralysis while still limiting downside?

### Learn and Be Curious
- Tell me about a time you had to quickly ramp up on an unfamiliar domain or technology to unblock a project. How did you approach the learning?
- Describe something you learned recently that changed how you approach a technical problem.

### Earn Trust
- Tell me about a time you gave a teammate or your manager critical feedback that was uncomfortable to deliver. How did you frame it?
- Describe a time you were wrong publicly and had to own it in front of the team. What did you do afterward to rebuild credibility?

### Dive Deep
- Tell me about a time metrics told one story and anecdotal reports told another. How did you resolve the discrepancy?
- Describe a time you found a root cause others had missed because they stopped investigating too early.
- Tell me about a time you had to get into low-level details (logs, code, data) that were technically "beneath" your role to solve a problem.

### Have Backbone; Disagree and Commit
- Tell me about a time you disagreed with your manager's or a senior stakeholder's technical decision. How did you make your case, and what happened?
- Describe a decision you disagreed with but committed to fully once it was made. How did you handle communicating that to your own team?

### Deliver Results
- Tell me about a time you delivered a critical result despite a major setback (an outage, a key teammate leaving, a scope change). Walk me through the recovery.
- Describe the highest-stakes deadline you've hit, and what you personally did (not the team) to make sure it landed.

### Hire and Develop the Best
- Tell me about a time you mentored someone and can point to a measurable change in their output or trajectory.
- Describe a time you raised the bar in a hiring or code-review process — what specifically did you change, and what was the before/after?

### Think Big
- Tell me about a time you proposed a solution bigger in scope than what was asked, and made the case for it.
- Describe a time your team was thinking incrementally and you pushed for a bolder direction. What was the outcome?

### Are Right, A Lot
- Tell me about a significant technical or product decision you got right despite pushback, where the data wasn't obviously on your side upfront.
- Describe a time you changed your mind on a strong initial opinion after new information — what moved you?

### (Gaps not in your worksheet — worth at least one story each)
**Frugality:** Tell me about a time you accomplished more with less (headcount, budget, infra spend) rather than requesting more resources.
**Strive to be Earth's Best Employer:** Tell me about a time you improved the work environment or process for your team, beyond your own output.
**Success and Scale Bring Broad Responsibility:** Tell me about a time you considered the broader (community, security, ethical) impact of a system you built, beyond its immediate function.

## How to use this with the worksheet

1. For each LP in your worksheet, write the story, **then check it against the L5 bar above**: does it show *you* scoping ambiguity (not receiving a scoped task)? Does it involve cross-team or cross-system impact? Is there a quantified result?
2. Write the two "Alternative Solutions" the worksheet asks for — these are your rehearsal for the "what would you do differently" follow-up that's explicitly called out as separating good from great answers.
3. Cross-reference: one strong story often covers 2–3 LPs (e.g., a production incident story can hit Ownership + Dive Deep + Deliver Results). Map your 8–10 stories against this whole list to find natural overlaps rather than writing 16 separate stories.
4. Practice the follow-up chain, not just the STAR delivery — interviewers go 3–5 questions deep on one story. After telling it once, ask yourself (or a partner) "why," "what if X had gone differently," and "what would you change" for each story.
